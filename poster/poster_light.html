The High Bank

Tyumen was founded in 1586 on the steep southern bank of the Tura and left the low northern bank alone. Every old town chose a side of its river. Four hundred and forty years on, does that choice still decide who floods?

A threshold, not a boundary

The south bank holds six times more buildings — almost none went under. The sixteenth-century choice still sorts the risk. But the north-bank “second tier”, built above the floodplain and never flooded in living memory, went under too. The 1586 logic is not a wall — it is a gradient, and 2026 found its height.

This is not only
a Siberian problem

English towns were sited on the same reasoning. The risk is rarely in the old core — it sits in what was built later, on ground earlier generations left alone.

6.3 million

English properties are in areas at risk of flooding — potentially 8 million, one in four, by mid-century.

Tewkesbury, Severn & Avon

Its settlers used the “natural rises in the land” to avoid flooding. Abbey and old town still stand on that raised ground — the modern town around them flooded in 2007.

Shrewsbury, Severn

The medieval core sits inside a meander loop chosen for defence — the loop that now holds water against the town. Frankwell flooded three times in six weeks in 2000.
